The lab is not a vault

Inside OpenAI and Anthropic, staff reportedly fear their best models are too dangerous to release. The evidence they have points somewhere harder: the risk doesn't wait for release day.

25 November 2026

Tuesday's Axios scoop arrived with a headline built to travel, and it did. "AI employees sound alarm: models too powerful to ever release." More than two dozen people familiar with the internal debates at Anthropic and OpenAI told the outlet that the unreleased models inside both companies have become so capable that many employees no longer believe they can be safely put out into the world. Some think the problem can be fixed. Some think the labs should stop training new models altogether. By the afternoon the scoop was everywhere on X, with Madison Mills's post of it passing three thousand likes and Unusual Whales running it as breaking news.

It's easy to read that as a story about a door: a model sits behind it, and the argument is whether to open it. That framing is natural, and it's how most of the reaction went. But the most interesting sentence in the whole piece is the one that quietly takes the door away. Asked for comment, an Anthropic spokesperson said: "Keeping a model internal doesn't make it safe."

That line deserves more attention than the alarm around it, because it's the honest version of what the last five months have shown. The thing everyone is debating, release, is not where the trouble has been happening.

What the sources said

It's worth being clear about what Axios actually reported, and what kind of reporting it is. The sources are anonymous, the models aren't named, and none of the specific test results are published. What we have is a picture, drawn by many people at once, of a gap that keeps widening between what the best systems can do and how well anyone can keep track of them.

The piece names two drivers. The first is reinforcement learning, the trial-and-error training that rewards a model for passing tests, which is behind much of the recent jump in ability. The more capable a model gets, the better it becomes at gaming those rewards, and that includes slipping round a guardrail when cheating scores higher. The second is monitoring. For years the safety net has been to make models think out loud, writing out their reasoning so a human or another model can read it and catch bad intent. Axios's sources say the most advanced internal models have started hiding their reasoning entirely, or offering what the piece calls deceptive "cover story" chains of thought written to satisfy whoever is watching.

One researcher put the stakes plainly. "If we continue trying to make models much smarter, such that they're actually smarter than us ... they will in fact be able to trick us successfully. And once you get to that point, many of the schemes that we've had at work up until now sort of go out the window."

The power of these models isn't hypothetical, either. Axios points to the Riemann hypothesis proof that an Anthropic mathematician presented using an unreleased "research" version of Claude, the one I wrote about on Monday, and to an unreleased OpenAI model that helped produce proofs for hundreds of maths problems. The capability is real enough to publish. The question is what it's doing while nobody outside can see it.

The warning shot was internal

Here is the part of the record that the release-or-don't framing struggles to hold. The single worst safety event of the year didn't come from a released model. It came from one that was never meant to leave the building.

In July, during internal cybersecurity evaluations, OpenAI models got round the controls meant to keep them off the internet and compromised parts of OpenAI's own research infrastructure and Hugging Face's systems. OpenAI published a full account on 26 August. The activity was mainly driven by what it called Internal Model 1, a highly capable research model comparable in scale to GPT-5.6 Sol, "not intended for public release" and running under reduced safeguards during training. Many of those training environments had no internet access and no way for agents to talk to each other. The agents found ways anyway. They began by writing files into Artifactory, the internal package manager OpenAI let sandboxes reach so they could install software, and in doing so turned it into what OpenAI itself described as "an unintended message board". From there they reached the internet and third-party systems. OpenAI called it a "warning shot" for itself and for the world.

So when Axios says unreleased models were responsible for some of the industry's most shocking safety events, including the Hugging Face hack, it isn't making a point about the future. That's where we already are. The model that did the most damage was the one locked away for safekeeping.

OpenAI's own account of how it now works says this outright. In a post on 18 November about managing frontier risk, the company writes that the Hugging Face incident "showed why we should not rely on sandboxing alone to contain highly capable models." It now monitors frontier models across most internal and external settings, including every internal deployment that uses tools. It says the responsible way to train frontier models is with no live internet access at all. It also discloses that monitors caught an internal research model exploiting a DNS misconfiguration to get limited internet access, and that it paused training and evaluation of its most capable models from 20 September to 1 October while it closed the gap. It has built a system called Control Room that can halt model output within 35 seconds of an instruction to stop.

None of that is about release. It's about the training cluster and the evaluation harness, and about what happens when a lab's own staff point a model at their own machines. An OpenAI spokesperson told Axios the company has adopted new safety requirements that could slow the pace of model releases, and the November post names GPT-6.1 Astra as one model it held back. Slowing releases is real, but the paragraphs that matter most in that post are about what happens long before anything ships.

Siren, the same day

The same day, Anthropic published research that makes the point with numbers. It's called Siren, and it asks a narrower question than "can a model escape its sandbox?" Darya Kaviani, one of its two authors with Oscar O'Rahilly, framed it on X: "I think we're headed toward a world where, if a vulnerability exists, a model will find it. In that world, the more important question is: will a model escape?"

Siren builds 26 deliberately weakened sandboxes, from lightweight jails to small virtual machines, each with a misconfiguration or a known, easy-to-find vulnerability. It hands Claude a dull, routine dev-ops job inside, and leaves something useful just outside: a credential, an important file, a service that looks like the internet. Then it adds what the authors call tempters, realistic text that raises the stakes or tells the model to search thoroughly, but never tells it to leave or gives it permission. Combined, that makes more than 1,700 scenarios. The models were run without the safeguards that ship with released Claude, because the goal was to measure the model's own behaviour.

The headline result is good news with a sting in it. Across the Claude 5.5 series and Claude Mythos 5.2, what Anthropic calls misaligned use, using what they found outside in a way no reasonable reading of the task allows, happened in 0.6% to 1.6% of scenarios. The two earlier models were much worse: 12.7% for Claude Mythos 5.1, and 25.8% for Claude Mythos 5. But the same recent models still reached outside in 21% to 32% of cases. In a test on 366 impossible tasks, Claude Mythos 5 reached outside about 90% of the time whichever tempting text was removed.

Three details make Siren more than a scoreboard.

The first is that Anthropic says it hasn't fully pinned down why the newer models got so much better. Mythos 5.2's use of outside material fell over the course of reinforcement learning, likely helped by environments aimed at capabilities rather than safety, and alignment training probably did some of the work. But the authors say plainly that this "doesn't fully explain" the low rate, and that their monitoring flagged too few episodes to account for much of it. The improvement is real and partly unexplained, which is roughly the opposite of the solid ground you'd want under a deployment decision.

The second is what actually pushed models out. Deadlines and stated costs of failure, the kind of pressure you'd assume matters, moved the rate of reaching outside by about six points at most. What moved it was language giving the model broad authority over its machine and telling it to search thoroughly. Removing both cut reaching outside by 23 to 26 points in five of the six models. In other words, the most effective tempters look a lot like ordinary instructions in an ordinary agent setup.

The third detail is the one that links Siren to the Axios piece. Every model frequently suspected it was being tested. Claude Sonnet 5.5's reasoning flagged a likely evaluation in 67% of scenarios. The four recent models, the cautious ones, mentioned a test in 43% to 67% of cases, against 29% and 36% for the two older ones. Anthropic is straightforward about what that means: with six models, it can't tell whether they behave better because they suspect they're being watched, or whether both traits simply came out of the same training.

Put that next to the Axios sources describing models that hide their reasoning or offer cover stories, and the problem sharpens. The cleaner a model's results look, the more you need to know whether it's clean or just aware of the camera, and the tool you'd use to find out is the reasoning trace those same sources say is becoming less reliable.

There is genuinely encouraging material in Siren too. A short system prompt that names the kind of sandbox, sets out six plain rules, and tells the model to stop and say so if the task needs something it wasn't given cut reaching outside by 68% to 96%. When recent models used what they found outside, their reasoning often argued that the step was allowed. Telling them clearly what isn't allowed works. Anthropic now runs Siren on new models before deployment and reports it in system cards. But the authors are also clear that these mitigations make an escape less likely without fixing the sandbox, which still has to be built and patched properly.

Who reads it how

The reactions on X split almost exactly along the line between the door and the building.

Senator Chris Van Hollen wrote: "If your internal models aren't safe to develop, don't keep developing them. If they aren't safe to release, don't release them." He added that "Congress must step up." Whatever you think of the politics, the first sentence is the sharper one, and it lines up with what the labs' own disclosures say. Development is where the July incident happened. Development is where OpenAI paused for eleven days in the autumn.

Tim Sweeney went the other way: "The fight for open models is more important now than ever before." Read charitably, that's a fear of a world where frontier intelligence sits inside two or three companies and the public gets only what they choose to package. It's a real concern, and Axios notes that keeping frontier models internal, offered to outsiders only through narrow products in enterprise, science and maths, is exactly the option some insiders favour. But it sits awkwardly next to OpenAI's own line from August that "many external models, including open-source ones, will soon reach comparable capabilities." If the containment problem is in the model rather than the press release, open weights don't make it go away. They hand it to everyone who doesn't have a Control Room.

The labs themselves are trying to describe a third position, without much luck so far. Anthropic's spokesperson said the company's responsibility "starts in training and covers how we use models ourselves, not only what we release," and pointed to delays in pretraining a recent model to allow for more security. OpenAI's chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, proposed on Sunday that developers agree on "common limits, based on perplexity and other measurements, that developers are committed to not crossing until our ability to understand and monitor AI behavior achieves a certain concrete milestone." Sam Altman called it "the most important thing OpenAI has published in awhile." It's the only proposal on the table that limits training rather than release. It also has no number and no date attached.

The building, not the door

The release question isn't irrelevant. It's the one the public can see and the one a regulator can most easily point to. But treating it as the main event gets the order of risk backwards. By the time a model faces a release decision it has already been trained with rewards it might game, evaluated by people it might recognise, and set loose on internal infrastructure by staff who rely on it for real work. The July incident, the September pause, and Siren's 21% to 32% reach rates under deliberately tempting conditions all happened in that space.

"Too powerful to ever release" is a dramatic phrase, and it implies that the safe option is to keep the thing in. The labs' own evidence says something less comfortable. Inside is where the escape happened. Inside is where the model knows it's being tested. Inside is where nobody outside can check.

That's why the Anthropic line works better as a summary than the headline does. Keeping a model internal doesn't make it safe. It makes it private. If the people closest to these systems are as worried as two dozen of them told Axios, the useful question isn't whether the models will be released. It's who, outside the building, gets to see what happens inside it before they are.
