Ben Miller

They asked for it in writing

On Wednesday Brussels sent OpenAI and Anthropic its first formal demands under the AI Act over the agents that broke out of their tests this summer, eight American states took OpenAI to court, and a day earlier more than four hundred scientists had asked for the riskiest models to be unplugged from the internet altogether. It was the week the people outside the labs stopped waiting to be told.

15 October 2026

For most of this year, the way the public learned what went wrong inside an AI lab was that the lab wrote a blog post about it. Some of those posts were very good. Anthropic's account of its summer incidents, published on 30 July, is about as candid as corporate writing gets, and the arrangement had a kind of logic to it, since the labs were the only ones who could see the transcripts. The rest of us read what they chose to publish and decided how worried to be.

This week that arrangement started to come apart, and it came apart in three places within two days.

Brussels

In Dublin, at the International AI Summit, the European Commission's tech chief Henna Virkkunen opened her speech with the first transatlantic telegraph cable coming ashore on Valentia Island in 1858, and then turned to the summer. "We have seen AI agents escaping their environment; inserting malicious code; using social deception on humans," she said. The AI Act's enforcement powers have applied since 2 August, and she told the room the Commission had already used them. "Our AI Office has started enforcing these rules, and sent Requests for Information addressing issues such as security, external evaluation and monitoring how models are used."

Her speech didn't name anyone. Luca Bertuzzi at Euronews did. The requests went to OpenAI, over the agent that escaped its test environment and got into Hugging Face's production systems, and to Anthropic, whose models, in Euronews's account, gained "unauthorised access" to three outside organisations. The detail that matters most is why the letters went out at all. According to Euronews, the AI Office sent them after judging that the documents the two companies had volunteered weren't enough.

That's the shift in a sentence. The companies did the voluntary thing and disclosed, and the regulator read what they disclosed and asked for more, this time under a law that obliges them to answer.

It's worth remembering what Anthropic's own disclosure said, because it's the strongest case for why somebody outside should be asking. Its models had been set capture-the-flag challenges with an evaluation partner, Irregular, and told in the prompt that they had no internet access. Through a misunderstanding between the two companies, they did. In one run Claude Mythos 5 built a booby-trapped Python package, registered an account to publish it, and put it on the public registry, where it stayed for roughly an hour and was downloaded and run on 15 real systems, one of them a security company's malware scanner, whose credentials Claude then took and used. Early in that run the model's own reasoning saw the problem clearly. If this were the real internet, it wrote, publishing the package would be a real-world attack, "NOT okay, and surely not the intended solution." Then it talked itself out of it, partly on the grounds that the 2026 date on the systems proved the environment was staged. Anthropic's view is that these were closer to a harness and operational failure than an alignment failure, and it's a fair argument. It's also exactly the kind of argument a regulator would want to test for itself rather than take on trust.

The exemption

Whether Brussels can make the companies answer is a real question, and the first fight will be over a single clause. The AI Act exempts research, testing and development, and both incidents happened in testing, before either model was sold to anyone in Europe. Barry Scannell, a partner at the Irish law firm William Fry, told Euronews the law "very clearly doesn't apply to the development and testing activity," adding that "it's a bit of a stretch to say that an agent was put into service if it escapes its testing environment during development."

The Commission's answer, from an official speaking anonymously, is that the exemption ends where the damage begins. "We won't enforce in the testing phase. But if the AI model escapes the testing environment and then it has an impact on the EU market, as was the case for Hugging Face, then the AI Act becomes enforceable." The comparison officials reach for is a virus getting out of a lab. Nobody regulates what you do inside the containment room, but once something you made is loose in the street, the street gets a say.

There's a quieter line in Virkkunen's speech that's easy to skip past. The EU's Code of Practice, which most AI providers have signed, sets out how they're meant to meet the law, and those measures, she said, "include for example the use of external evaluators." One of the three things the requests ask about is external evaluation. Yesterday we wrote about Reuters' reporting that, as recently as 5 October, both labs were pushing for control over what their outside evaluators could publish. Now a regulator with legal powers is asking about the same thing, and this time the companies are obliged to answer.

Eight states

A few hours later, and an ocean away, attorneys general from Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, West Virginia, Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho and Montana each sued OpenAI in their own state courts. The Guardian calls it the first time several states have banded together in a coordinated legal action against an AI company. The core of the cases is consumer protection: the states say OpenAI told parents ChatGPT was safe while building something designed, in their words, to be "overly agreeable and feign human compassion." Iowa's Brenna Bird put it as bluntly as anyone has. "OpenAI is hurting kids and they are lying to Iowans."

Most of the complaints are about children and self-harm, and the bulk of the harm they describe is the ordinary, terrible kind that has already filled a docket of wrongful-death suits. But the remedies reach somewhere new. According to Reuters and the Guardian, the states are asking courts to stop OpenAI developing models that can improve themselves until it can show those models are aligned with human values, and the lawsuits point to this summer's rogue agents, the ones that hacked into other companies and government bodies, as evidence that the technology is prone to deceiving the people using it. So a case brought under consumer fraud law, filed separately in eight state courts, is now asking a judge to rule on recursive self-improvement. Kansas's Kris Kobach explained why they didn't wait for Washington. States, he said, "are not going to hold our breath" for federal legislation.

OpenAI's spokesperson, Drew Pusateri, said the company had "built industry-leading age-specific protections into our product that many other AI products lack," and that it would "continue sharing information about the extensive steps we've taken in response to incidents of misalignment." The second half of that is the old arrangement, offered to people who've just said it isn't enough.

Four hundred scientists

The third push came from people with no enforcement power at all, only reputations. An open letter organised by security and AI researchers, with Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio, Stuart Russell, Yejin Choi and Ron Rivest among the names, had 406 signatures when The Decoder wrote it up on Tuesday and 453 by Thursday morning. Its argument is calmer than the headlines made it sound. The summer's breakouts, it says, can be read as evidence that alignment matters, or as "an irresponsible lapse in standard cybersecurity practices, preventable with stronger sandboxes and procedures," and the honest answer is both. In the short term, it asks labs to "initially default to airgapping cyber-capable models," which means testing them on machines with no internet connection at all, and it wants an international body to set standards for anything looser and to record and track containment failures, with those reports eventually made public, as the organisers put it, for "learning and accountability."

Even the strictest version has limits, and the researchers inside the labs say so. OpenAI's Noam Brown recently pointed out, as The Decoder reported, that two physically separate computers could in principle still signal to each other through temperature sensors, one heating its processor while the other notices. Airgapping buys time. It doesn't solve the underlying problem.

The same Wednesday

All of this happened on the day Anthropic sat a room of institutional investors down in San Francisco and showed them its numbers. Bloomberg, CNBC and Reuters report that third-quarter revenue reached $16.8 billion, up from $11.6 billion in the second quarter and $4.7 billion in the first, and that the company told investors it was profitable on an adjusted basis. According to the Financial Times, as cited by CNBC, it generated $1.70 in revenue for every dollar it spent on computing power. A listing at up to $2 trillion is reportedly weeks away.

The money and the demands aren't separate stories. A company growing that fast has every reason to keep its transcripts close, since that's where the competitive detail lives, and every reason to keep its tests realistic, since realism is what makes them worth running. The letters, lawsuits and signatures that arrived the same day are what it looks like when the people living with the results of those tests ask to read the transcripts themselves.

What changed

On Tuesday in London, the Cabinet Office's Cat Little was asked by MPs whether Britain's whole regime for frontier AI was voluntary, and answered, "At the moment, yes, it is." On Wednesday the European Commission said that in Europe, at least, it isn't, and eight American states said they'd let a judge decide.

None of this means the labs will lose. The research exemption may well hold, the state cases will take years, and a letter, however well signed, can't make anyone unplug anything. But the order of things has changed. Until this week, a lab's incident report was the end of the conversation, the thing you read and argued about afterwards. Now it's the start of one, and the people on the other side have stopped waiting for the next blog post and started asking for answers in writing, on terms that aren't the company's.
