Ben Miller

The rule arrived before the form

Nvidia's pause on server shipments to four Southeast Asian countries isn't a US ban. It's what happens when Washington tells companies what they must know about their customers and never says what knowing looks like.

27 November 2026

The news reached most people on X the way breaking news usually does now, as a single line in capital letters. "BREAKING: NVIDIA has halted shipments of its chips to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia," the Spectator Index posted early on Thursday, and by Friday morning more than 800,000 people had seen it. Watcher Guru put the four countries in a bulleted list, as if it were a ruling. By the small hours of Friday someone with a large following had worked out what it meant, or thought he had: "~50% of Nvidia's revenue just evaporated."

It didn't, and we'll come to why. But the instinct behind that post, that something large had just been decided, was right. What the one-liners left out is who decided it. Washington did not ban anything on Thursday. Nvidia did, and it did so because the rule Washington wrote a week earlier tells companies what they must know without telling them what knowing looks like.

What actually happened

The reporting comes from the Financial Times, which broke it on Thursday, and the Wall Street Journal, which reported the same instruction separately. Nvidia has told the companies that build its chips into servers, Dell and Super Micro among them, to stop shipping those servers to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia. According to the FT's three sources, Dell and Super Micro have told buyers in those countries that Nvidia will not let their orders go out. The Journal says the pause is for an unspecified period while Nvidia checks whether its partners in the region meet US export requirements. Neither paper could say whether it is temporary. Nvidia declined to comment.

The rule behind it is an interim final rule from the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security, issued on Monday 16 November and in force from Wednesday 19 November. It does two things. First, it adds five countries, Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, to the list of places where shipping advanced AI chips needs a licence, which BIS says it will review "case-by-case". Buyers headquartered in the United States are exempt, as long as the exporter files monthly reports. Second, and much more ambitious, it makes it a licensable export to sell chips to a data centre anywhere in the world if the exporter knows that data centre will rent a large amount of computing power to companies headquartered in China, Macau or other countries under a US arms embargo.

"Large" turns out to be not very large at all. The threshold is 12 million units of what the rule calls total processing performance. The analysts Kate Koren and Kevin Kurland at CSIS put that at roughly 750 H200s, or around 42 of Nvidia's new Vera Rubin systems. That is a mid-sized cluster, not a hyperscale campus. And because the rule controls what the hardware is used for rather than the moment it is sold, it reaches back to servers that already shipped. Data centres that were renting to Chinese customers on 19 November have 90 days, which takes us to 17 February, to get a licence or stop.

So far this is a government making policy. The odd part is what happened next.

The missing paragraph

A licence regime needs two things: a rule saying when you need a licence, and a process for getting one. Washington has published the first. According to everyone trying to comply with it, it has not published the second.

"There's this new rule but no guidance on execution," an executive at a cloud provider told the FT. "No one knows how to apply for this licence while the 90-day clock is ticking. It's causing sheer chaos in the region."

The CSIS analysts saw this coming two days after the rule was issued. For new shipments, they wrote, exporters have to gather enough information from foreign data centres to be sure those centres have done proper know-your-customer checks. But "the rule does not provide details on what sufficient information means in this context, which may lead to overcompliance, with companies restricting sales to avoid risk."

That sentence, published on 18 November, describes Thursday almost exactly. When the legal test turns on what you know, and nobody will tell you how much checking counts as knowing, the safest amount of knowledge is all of it, and the safest way to get there is to stop shipping until you have it. The rule set the standard. Nvidia, which sees more of the global AI hardware market than any regulator does, picked the strictest reading of it and applied that reading to four entire countries at once.

It is worth being exact about what that means. Nothing in the rule bans sales to Singapore. Singapore already has some of the strongest export controls in Asia, and CSIS pointed out that how well a destination polices its own exports is one of the things BIS will weigh when it reviews a licence. US-headquartered buyers in all five countries are exempt by design. None of the reporting says whether Nvidia's pause spares them. Brazil is on the rule's list and missing from the halt. In other words, the line that now matters in Southeast Asia is not the one Commerce drew. It is a broader one, drawn in Santa Clara, and nobody outside Nvidia knows when it moves.

Why it would be Nvidia

You could read this as a company being cautious, and partly it is. Nvidia has spent much of 2026 being told that its chips keep turning up where they shouldn't. In March federal prosecutors charged three people connected to Super Micro, including its co-founder Wally Liaw, with conspiring to smuggle AI chips to China. Super Micro was not charged and Liaw has pleaded not guilty, but two senators, Jim Banks and Elizabeth Warren, then asked Commerce to consider freezing active licences for Nvidia chips headed to China and to middlemen in Malaysia, Thailand, Vietnam and Singapore. The FT has reported that Nvidia cut more than half of its authorised Asian buyers in July after bringing in a customer whitelist and sending staff to inspect data centres in Singapore, Malaysia and Japan.

Then came November. Jensen Huang resigned on 12 November after reports of extramarital affairs, one of them with Alice Huang, a former executive of Megaspeed, a Singapore cloud company that the Justice Department and Commerce said on 16 November they are investigating over possible diversion of chips to China. US officials have since told congressional staffers they found no evidence she was directed to spy on him, though they remain divided over her contacts with Chinese intelligence. The new rule came out the same day the investigation was announced. Nvidia's board brought Huang back on 23 November, on what it called "input from senior levels of the United States government". On 1 December he is due to sit a closed-door interview with three House committees. The Senate Banking chairman, Tim Scott, has asked him to appear on 3 December.

The FT's sources said many in the region had hoped Huang's return would mean the new rules got enforced more gently. There is "little indication" of that. Put yourself in his place and it isn't hard to see why. A chief executive who is about to answer questions from Congress about a Singapore cloud company has every reason to arrive able to say his company stopped shipping to Singapore before anyone made it. The pause costs Nvidia revenue in the short run. It buys something it needs more right now, which is the look of a company that is policing itself harder than the government asked.

That is the part I find worth sitting with. A week ago this blog argued that Nvidia's board had settled the question of its chief executive by taking a reference from the White House rather than answering the question itself. This week the arrangement runs the other way. Washington has written a rule with a hole where the procedure should be, and Nvidia has filled the hole with the bluntest tool it has. Each side gets what it wants. The government gets a near-total stop on shipments to the region's neoclouds without having to sign, defend or answer for a ban. Nvidia gets to show good behaviour on the eve of its hearings. Nobody in Kuala Lumpur or Jakarta gets anyone to appeal to.

Meanwhile in Washington

It doesn't help that the administration is saying two things at once. The rule took effect on the same day Donald Trump met Xi Jinping in Shenzhen. The next day, the Treasury Secretary, Scott Bessent, told CNBC that the H200 is still the "threshold level" of chip that can be sold to China, that "that continues as planned", and that he wouldn't anticipate any change in the policy. Trump said the same afternoon that he and Xi had talked about how much they both like Jensen Huang. "He said, 'Oh, brilliant, brilliant! He's a brilliant man,'" Trump told reporters.

Read only those remarks and you would think export policy towards China had just held steady, or even softened. Read the rule and you would think it had tightened sharply. Read Thursday's reports and you find that the actual tightening on the ground is tighter still, because the company in the middle cannot tell which of those signals is the one it will be judged by, and is taking no chances. When officials send mixed messages, the cautious reading becomes the law, whoever wrote it.

Who feels it

The four countries did not become chip hotspots by accident. US restrictions on selling Nvidia's best chips to China pushed Alibaba, ByteDance and others to rent computing power abroad instead, and Southeast Asia was the nearest place with land, power and a welcome. In October the Institute for Progress estimated that about 75% of China's overseas AI computing would be in Southeast Asia by the end of this year. The Journal, citing the same think tank, puts remote data centres like these at about 24% of China's total AI computing power.

Malaysia has the most to lose. More than half of its data centre capacity is in shared colocation facilities, and Chinese firms account for "a sizeable share of these workloads", Capital Economics' Leah Fahy told CNBC. Its trade minister, Johari Abdul Ghani, has said Malaysia will tell all 117 approved data centre projects what the requirements are once Washington has explained them. His other line is the one people will remember: "If you want to be in the game, you have to follow U.S. regulations." Thailand's vice minister for commerce, Kirida Bhaopichitr, warned that new projects that cannot get US approval for advanced chips may "not be able to complete construction". Singapore says it will negotiate "trusted access" for its companies.

The money has already moved. Bloomberg reported that bankers froze work on a loan of around $480 million (the FT says about $500 million) to Aolani, a Singapore cloud company, to buy Nvidia chips for a Malaysian data centre that ByteDance planned to rent. The FT says ByteDance may now drop the deal. Earlier this month, the FT reported, Oracle cancelled a multibillion-dollar arrangement that would have given Tencent access to about 100,000 advanced AI chips. The Information reported last week that Moonshot had scrapped a $150 million deal to use Blackwell chips through an Indonesian data centre. CNBC reports dealers listing thousands of B300 chips that had been meant for data centres serving Chinese renters.

And then there are the labs. Reuters reported on Thursday that Z.AI, also known as Zhipu, had planned to release its next flagship, GLM-5.5, this month and still hasn't. The model is roughly 3 trillion parameters, and one source said it has been held up partly by better safeguards against distillation, the practice of training a model on answers from a stronger one. The new rule could push its launch into the first quarter of 2027 or later. Moonshot, which rents its training compute and prefers overseas providers, internally expects the six months between its major releases to stretch to a year or more if it loses that access, with Kimi K4 slipping to around the middle of 2027. One detail in that report deserves its own headline: with OpenAI and Anthropic harder to distil from, Moonshot has started distilling from Google and Meta models instead. Neither company had responded when Reuters published.

If you wanted evidence that compute restrictions work, there it is. Whether they work for the reason intended is another question.

Where the compute goes instead

Look again at the exemption for US-headquartered buyers. A Chinese lab that can no longer rent from a neocloud in Johor can, in principle, still rent from an American cloud company, possibly one with a data centre in the same country, and possibly one at home. Chris McGuire, a former National Security Council official now at the Council on Foreign Relations, put it bluntly: "There is a significant risk that Chinese companies will pivot from renting compute from data centers in Southeast Asia to renting compute from data centers in Virginia." Moonshot is already shopping in Europe.

So the rule's practical effect, at least for now, is less a wall around China than a reshuffle of who is allowed to sell to it. Smaller, local cloud companies in Southeast Asia, which were the whole point of the region's boom, are frozen out by a shipping pause that nobody has said will end. The American hyperscalers, who qualify for the exemption and have the compliance departments to file monthly reports, become the safe route. Analysts told CNBC that US cloud giants "may get a modest boost". That might be good policy. It is also a significant decision about the shape of a market, and it is being made through a missing procedure and a private company's caution rather than in any document anyone signed.

About that 50%

Back to the post that said half of Nvidia's revenue had just evaporated. The figure it was gesturing at is real. Nvidia booked $104 billion of revenue through its Singapore subsidiary in 2025, up 72% in a year, according to company accounts cited by the FT. But that number shows where sales are billed, not where chips end up. The FT notes that Nvidia books some of its Taiwan and China revenue through the Singapore unit. And in its fiscal year that ended in January 2025, Singapore was 18% of Nvidia's revenue by billing location and under 2% by products actually shipped there. Treating a billing address as a delivery address is how a pause on four countries turns into the end of half a company on X.

None of this means the pause is cheap for Nvidia. Malaysia alone has drawn close to $100 billion of data centre investment since 2021, according to Reuters, and much of it was built to hold Nvidia hardware. US markets were shut for Thanksgiving when the story broke, so today's half-day session is the first chance American investors have had to price it. But the useful question isn't how big the hole is. It is who decides when it closes.

What to watch

There are four dates and one document.

The document is the licence procedure for data centres. Until BIS publishes it, the 90-day clock runs against a form that doesn't exist, and Nvidia's reading of the rule is the only one that counts. If the procedure arrives soon and is workable, Thursday will look like a sensible pause. If it doesn't, the pause becomes a ban by default.

The dates are 1 December, when Huang sits down with the House committees; 3 December, if the Senate Banking hearing goes ahead; 19 January 2027, when the public comment period closes, according to a summary from the law firm Greenberg Traurig; and 17 February, when data centres that were renting to Chinese customers must have a licence or stop. Somewhere in there Nvidia will either lift the pause, narrow it to the buyers it can't vouch for, or leave it standing. It will most likely do that without saying why.

Export controls are supposed to be the clearest kind of state power: a government decides what may leave its borders and to whom, publishes the rule, and answers for it. What happened this week is a version where the government decides how much it wants to know and the company decides what that means. That arrangement may well slow China down. Reuters' sources suggest it already is. But it leaves Malaysia's 117 projects, a Singapore cloud company's $500 million loan and the release schedules of two Chinese labs hanging on a compliance call in Santa Clara, at a moment when the person who signs off on it has very good reasons of his own to be strict.

The rule arrived. The form didn't. Until it does, the people who sell the chips are the ones deciding who gets them.
