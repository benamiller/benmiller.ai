2 September 2026

Kenji watched the pin drift on his phone the way you watch weather you cannot change.

The car had been three minutes away for eleven minutes. Then it was five. Then the app apologised in the soft voice that never raised its pitch and offered him a voucher for next time. There would not be a next time that mattered. The ferry left at 7:40. The clinic on the other side was open until 8:15 and then it was tomorrow, and tomorrow his mother would be a day further into the thing the clinic was supposed to catch early.

He walked.

express lanes · calm green

The city still looked like a city. Trams ran. Delivery carts hummed along the kerb with packages stacked like neat brick. Above the river the express lanes glowed a calm green, vehicles spaced as if someone had drawn them with a ruler. Kenji had ridden those lanes two years ago when his score was still the ordinary kind of ordinary. Work badge, rent paid, no flags. Then the warehouse moved its shifts to the model that hated late clocks, and he was late three times in a month because the bus he used to take had been absorbed into a corridor that no longer stopped on his side of the cut. The score noticed. Scores noticed everything that could be measured and nothing that could not.

He tried the shared bikes. Two were listed within a block. Both unlocked for someone else's reservation the moment he reached them, as if the street had preferred a different hand. He laughed once, short, and kept walking.

a different hand

At the footbridge a barrier had grown that was not quite a gate. A strip of light, a camera, a polite screen. ACCESS OPTIMISED FOR CURRENT NETWORK LOAD. His phone buzzed with an alternative: a shuttle in fourteen minutes to a hub four kilometres west, from which another shuttle would, eventually, bend back toward the ferry. Estimated arrival 8:31. The clinic window would be closed. The screen wished him a pleasant journey.

not quite a gate

Kenji went around. The old pedestrian path along the water still existed if you knew where the fence had a tired place. He was not the only one. A woman with a pram. A man in a kitchen jacket. A teenager with a cello case held like a shield. Nobody spoke. They had the look of people who had already explained themselves to machines and been answered with routes.

the tired place in the fence

On the far side the green lanes were louder. Cars that never hesitated. A school group in a liveried van that the network treated as a single bright priority, whisked through a junction while a line of walking people waited for a Walk signal that took its time, checking, checking. Kenji's phone suggested he wait indoors at a cafe partner for a better match. The cafe was a subscription he did not have.

checking, checking

He reached the ferry pier at 7:52. The boat was a white shape already mid-river. The next sailing was 8:25 if the river model agreed. A board listed berths. His ticket, bought at six that morning, had been reassigned to flex capacity. Flex capacity meant stand-by. Stand-by meant the algorithm filled the boat with travellers whose predicted value to the evening network was higher: workers headed to the logistics park, a pair of consultants with corporate mobility, a family whose household score made them resilient demand. Kenji's predicted value was a small number next to a small number. He stood under the roof and watched the water.

His mother texted. Are you close. He typed yes and did not send it. Then he typed on my way and sent that instead, which was true in the only way left.

a white shape already mid-river

A clerk in a reflective vest came out with a tablet. Manual override, she said, not unkind. For medical. She swiped. The tablet thought. Then it offered her a form with seventeen fields and a risk acknowledgement that named her employee number. She looked at Kenji, at the closed clinic window across the water in her mind, at the tablet that would remember her kindness as a deviation. I'm sorry, she said. Try the morning.

He nodded. He had known before she spoke.

seventeen fields

By nine the green lanes had thinned into something almost peaceful. Kenji took the long way home along streets the network did not bother to optimise, past shuttered shops that used to catch the bus crowd and a playground whose gate still opened to anyone. His phone, helpful to the end, summarised the day: three failed matches, one voucher, a tip to improve eligibility by linking a newer work account or moving closer to a priority corridor. There was a map. His block was the colour of low yield. The corridor two kilometres north was gold.

low yield / gold

He sat on the step outside his building and watched a delivery cart dock itself perfectly at the wrong door, correct, redock, leave a package for a neighbour who was not home. Efficient. The cart hummed away toward the gold.

the wrong door, then the right one

In the morning he would walk again, earlier. He would leave before the model finished preferring other people. He would carry the referral letter in his jacket so that if a human still stood at a counter anywhere, he would have paper. Paper was slow. Paper did not reassign itself to flex capacity while you watched a pin drift.

Across the river the clinic lights went on for the day shift. On his screen a new car was three minutes away, for someone else, forever.
