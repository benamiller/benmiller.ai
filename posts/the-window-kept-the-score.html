The window kept the score

30 October 2026

Denise Ablewhite had been a coroner's officer in Boston for nineteen years, and for most of them the job had been what she told people at parties it was, which was mostly sitting in kitchens. Somebody died at home, alone or not alone, and the police came, and then she came, and she sat at the table with whoever was left and asked them the questions that the coroner would want answered, and she wrote the answers down in a hand that her sergeant used to say looked like a fence in a gale. Who found him. When did you last speak. Was he on anything. Did he have a doctor. Most of the time the answers were ordinary and the death was ordinary, and the coroner signed it off, and Denise went back to the office on Lincoln Lane and had a cup of tea that she let go cold while she typed it all up. She liked the work more than she could explain to anyone who didn't do it. You were the person who turned up on the worst day and didn't flinch, and people remembered that, and sometimes years later a woman would stop her in the Pescod Square car park and say you were the one who came for my dad, and Denise would say yes, I was, and they'd stand there for a minute holding their bags.

The heat changed the kitchens before it changed anything else. That was how she thought of it afterwards. The first proper bad summer, the one in 2031 when it touched forty at Coningsby and the tarmac on the A16 went soft enough to keep a bootprint, she did eleven heat deaths in nine days, and every one of them was in a kitchen or a front room or a bedroom with the curtains drawn, and every one of them was over seventy-five, and the families all said the same thing in slightly different words, which was that he wouldn't have the window open, or she didn't like the fan, or he said he'd been through 1976 and this was nothing. Denise wrote it all down. The coroner then was a man called Mr. Furlong who wore half-moon glasses and had been in the job since before she was, and he read every one of her reports and put most of them down as natural causes contributed to by environmental heat, and he said to her once, in the corridor, that he didn't know what else to call it, because the law didn't have a word for the weather killing you.

By 2034 the law had a word, and the word came with a number.

The Climate Loss Act had gone through in the spring of 2033, after two summers that nobody wanted to repeat and an inquiry into a care home in Rotherham that had made the evening news every night for a fortnight. It set up the National Adaptation Fund, which was paid for out of the carbon border levy and a slice of the windfall tax on the big generators, and it said, in a clause that Denise later knew by heart, that the next of kin of any person whose death was certified as heat-attributable during a declared heat event would receive a single payment of thirty-eight thousand pounds, together with a letter of acknowledgement from the Secretary of State. People called it the acknowledgement money. The letter was on cream paper with a blind-embossed crest, and it said that the government recognised that your loved one had died as a consequence of a changing climate, and that it was sorry. Denise had seen a lot of those letters framed. She had seen one on a mantelpiece in Kirton next to a wedding photo and a china spaniel.

The certification was done by Proximate. That was what the Act called it, the proximate attribution service, and that was what everyone called it, though the woman from the Ministry of Justice who came up to train them in the July before the first season kept saying "the service" as though it were a bus. Proximate was run by a company in Reading under contract to the Department for Environment, and it sat on a server somewhere that nobody Denise knew had ever seen, and what it did was read everything there was to read about a person's last days and return a percentage. It read the smart meter and the thermostat, if there was one. It read the Met Office grid for that postcode, hour by hour, and the satellite heat map for the street, and the building's energy certificate, and the GP record and the prescriptions, and the care plan if there was a care plan, and the call log from the council's heat line, and the doorbell camera if the family consented, which most of them did, because they'd been told it would help. It read the window sensors. Every social tenancy and every home that had taken a retrofit grant since 2030 had window sensors, little white plastic tabs on the frames, put in so that the ventilation could be monitored for damp. Proximate read those too.

Then it worked out, out of a hundred, how much of the death was the heat, and how much was everything else, and the line was seventy. Seventy and over was heat-attributable. Under seventy was natural causes, and the family got a letter as well, on ordinary paper, explaining that the service had determined that environmental heat was a contributory but not a principal factor, and that the government's thoughts were with them.

Denise's job had changed shape around this without anyone quite deciding that it should. She still went to the kitchens. She still asked the questions and wrote down the answers. But now there was a tablet in a rubber case that she had to fill in as well, with drop-down boxes, and when she had finished the tablet went off to Proximate, and three or four days later the number came back, and it was Denise who rang the family to tell them. Nobody had written that into her job description. It was just that the number came to the coroner's office, and the coroner signed it, and somebody had to ring, and the families had her card already, because she was the one who'd sat in the kitchen. So she rang. She got good at it. She got good at saying the number first and then saying the other things, because she had learned that if you said the other things first people could hear the number coming in your voice and it was worse.

The summer of 2034 came in early. There was a fortnight at the end of June when it sat at thirty-four and thirty-five and never went under twenty-two at night, and the Met Office declared the first heat event on the twenty-third, and Denise did six in the first week. Four came back over seventy. She rang those four with something that wasn't happiness but sat in the same part of her, a kind of relief on their behalf, and one of the daughters cried on the phone and said thank you, as if Denise had done it, and Denise said it wasn't her, love, it was the service, and the daughter said well thank you anyway. Two came back under. One at sixty-eight and one at fifty-one. The fifty-one was a man in his sixties with a bad heart who'd been drinking, and Denise could see how that might go, and she rang his brother and said it plainly and the brother said fair enough, he'd been a silly sod, and that was that.

The sixty-eight she thought about for longer. It was a woman called Pat Holland on the Fenside estate, eighty-three, who'd been found by her home help on the Monday morning in the chair by the television with the heating on. The heating had been on because the thermostat was set to twenty-one all year round and nobody had told her to change it, or somebody had and she'd forgotten, and the flat had gone to thirty-six degrees by the Sunday night with all four windows shut. Proximate had produced a short summary to go with the number, which it always did, in a font that was meant to look friendly, and the summary said that the deceased's indoor environment had been substantially elevated by heating use and the absence of ventilation during the declared event, and that these behavioural factors had been weighted as a non-environmental contribution in accordance with the published methodology. Denise read it twice. Then she read the published methodology, which was a sixty-page PDF on the Defra website, and on page thirty-one there was a section called "Occupant Contribution," and it said that where a person had the means to reduce their own heat exposure and had not done so, the portion of the heat burden attributable to that choice would be assessed as occupant-attributable and not climate-attributable. There was a worked example with a diagram of a house.

She rang Pat Holland's son, who lived in Leicester, and told him sixty-eight, and he was quiet for a long time, and then he said, so it's her fault, then, and Denise said no, that wasn't what it said, and he said what does it say then, and she found that she couldn't put it any other way.

Walter Sneath died on the eleventh of August, in the third heat event of the summer, which was the long one. It went on for nineteen days. At its worst it reached thirty-nine point six at Coningsby and thirty-eight in the middle of Boston, in the Market Place, where the electronic sign on the side of the Assembly Rooms that normally gave the time and the date and the price of parking had been given over to the temperature and a little cartoon sun and the words LOOK AFTER EACH OTHER. The river went down so far that the mud stood out along the Haven like a seam of liver, and it stank, and the gulls walked about on it. The council opened the library and the leisure centre and the big Tesco as cool rooms, and the queue outside the leisure centre went round the car park in the mornings, and people sat in the sports hall on fold-out chairs under the basketball hoops watching the clock.

Walter didn't go. Denise knew a fair bit about Walter by the time she'd finished, because his daughter Lorraine talked, and because the flat talked, the way they do. He was eighty-one. He had been born in a terraced house on Witham Bank and his father had been an eel-catcher, one of the last on that stretch of the river, and Walter had gone out with him in the flat-bottomed boat as a boy and then gone into the docks and then gone to the frozen-pea factory at Wyberton when the docks went quiet, and he'd done thirty-three years there on the line and then in the stores. He'd been married to a woman called Joan who had died in 2027, of a cancer, slowly, in the hospice at Pilgrim, and since then he'd lived on his own in a ground-floor flat in a council block off Tunnard Street. He had a budgie that he'd named after a darts player. He had an allotment until his knees went. He went to the Stump every Sunday, not for the service but for the cafe afterwards, because he liked the scones, and a woman there called Margaret used to keep one back for him.

He didn't open his windows because of the lad upstairs. That was what Lorraine told Denise, sitting at Walter's kitchen table on the twelfth of August with the windows open now and the air coming in still hot and thick off the street. There had been a break-in in the block in the winter of 2032, not at Walter's but at the flat across the landing, where an old woman called Mrs. Overton lived, and someone had come in through the back bedroom window while she slept and taken her purse and her late husband's medals and a tin of shortbread, and Mrs. Overton had gone into a home a month later and never come out. After that Walter kept the windows shut. All of them, all year. He had a little hook-and-eye catch that he'd fitted himself on top of the proper lock, on every frame, and at night he checked them like a man on a ship. Lorraine had told him and told him. The council heat line had rung him three times in the first week of the event, and the call log showed that he'd told them he was fine, he was keeping cool, he had a fan. He did have a fan. It was in the hall cupboard, still in its box, with the price label from Wilko on it, and Wilko had been gone eleven years.

Denise wrote it all down. She took photographs of the hooks and eyes. She took a photograph of the fan in its box. On the tablet she filled in the drop-downs, and when she got to the one that asked whether there was evidence that the deceased had chosen not to ventilate the property, the options were Yes, No, and Unknown, and she sat with her finger over the screen for a while, and then she pressed Unknown, because she didn't think chose was the word for what Walter had done, and because she didn't know what the word was.

It made no difference. The window sensors had already told Proximate that every window in the flat had been closed continuously from the fourteenth of July until Lorraine opened them on the morning she found him, and Proximate had the tablet's Unknown and the sensors' twenty-eight days of Closed, and it weighed them up, and on the fifteenth of August the number came back sixty-four.

The summary said that the deceased had declined three offers of support from the council's heat-response line and had maintained a fully unventilated indoor environment throughout the declared event, despite a documented ability to ventilate, and that the resulting elevation of indoor temperature over outdoor ambient had been assessed as occupant-attributable in accordance with section 4.3 of the published methodology.

Denise read it at her desk on Lincoln Lane with the blinds down and the office fan turning its head back and forth like it was looking for someone. Then she went and found Mr. Furlong's successor, who was a younger woman called Ms. Achebe-Ward who had come over from Nottingham and was good at her job and tired, and she put the printout on her desk and said, I don't think this is right.

Ms. Achebe-Ward read it. She read it properly, Denise would give her that. Then she took her glasses off and rubbed the marks on her nose and said that she didn't think it was right either, if by right Denise meant fair, but that it was correct, which was a different thing, and that she had no power to change it. The Act gave the coroner the power to certify the cause of death. It did not give her the power to change the attribution percentage, which was a matter for the service, and appeals against the attribution percentage went to the Adaptation Fund Review Panel, which sat in Leeds.

"So we can appeal," Denise said.

"The family can appeal."

"Then I'll tell Lorraine to appeal."

Ms. Achebe-Ward said that was her right, and then she said, in a different voice, that Denise should look at the panel's figures before she did, so that Lorraine knew what she was walking into, and then she put her glasses back on and picked up the next file, which was a drowning at Freiston Shore.

The panel's figures were on the Fund's website, under a tab marked Transparency. Denise looked at them that night at home, at her own kitchen table, with a glass of white wine going warm by her elbow and the back door open onto the yard and the sound of next door's air-con unit chugging through the fence. Her husband Gary was asleep on the settee with the cricket on. In the year to the end of June the panel had received four thousand one hundred and twelve appeals and upheld two hundred and nine of them. In every one of the upheld cases, which were summarised in a table with the names taken out, the reason given was a data error: a wrong postcode, a smart meter that had been reading someone else's flat, a care plan that belonged to a different man with the same name and date of birth. There was not one case in which the panel had found that the service had weighed the right data the wrong way. Denise read the panel's terms of reference, which were also on the website, and they said that the panel's role was to determine whether the service had correctly applied the published methodology to accurate data. It was not to determine whether the methodology was right. That was a matter for the Secretary of State.

She scrolled further down the Transparency page because she couldn't stop herself, and that was where she found the other thing.

It was in a quarterly report, a PDF, and it was dull in the way that the important things often were. It said that the Adaptation Fund's liabilities for the 2034 season were tracking above actuarial forecast owing to the duration and intensity of the second and third declared heat events, and that, in line with the Fund's sustainability mechanism as set out in Schedule 3 of the Act, the service's attribution threshold would be reviewed monthly against the Fund's available balance and adjusted where necessary to ensure the Fund's continued solvency for the remainder of the season. There was a little table at the bottom. It showed the threshold by month. April seventy. May seventy. June seventy. July seventy-two. August seventy-four, with an asterisk, and the asterisk said provisional.

Denise sat back. Gary turned over on the settee and said something about Jimmy Anderson, who had retired a decade ago. She looked at the table again. Walter's number was sixty-four, so it didn't matter, not for Walter, not this time. But Pat Holland had been sixty-eight in June, under seventy. And in July Denise had rung a family in Swineshead and told them seventy-one, over the line, and the woman had sobbed thank you down the phone, and she went and found that case in her own notebook, because she'd started keeping a notebook in July for reasons she couldn't have named. Seventy-one. Rung on the ninth of July. She went back to the PDF. The July threshold had applied from the first of the month.

She went into the office early the next day and pulled the Swineshead file and looked at what had actually been sent. The family had received the ordinary letter. On ordinary paper. The service had determined that environmental heat was a contributory but not a principal factor. Denise had told them seventy-one and over the line, because over the line was what seventy-one had meant when she learned the job, and nobody had told her that the line had moved, and the woman had sobbed thank you, and then a week later she must have opened the letter.

Denise rang her. She didn't know what she was going to say, and in the end she just said it, and the woman in Swineshead, whose name was Carol Dring and whose husband had been a lorry driver for Fowler Welch, listened all the way through and then said, I know, love. I rang up about it. They said the number was right but the line had changed. She said she'd thought about ringing Denise to tell her and then thought, what's the point, it's not your fault. Then she said, it's the not knowing what he was, that's what gets me. Am I a widow because it was hot or am I a widow because he was fat. Denise said she didn't think it was either of those, and Carol Dring said well, the government thinks it's mostly one and a bit of the other, and they've got a computer, so.

Lorraine Sneath appealed. Denise helped her fill in the form, at Walter's kitchen table, the week after the funeral, which had been at the crematorium on Marian Road with the windows of the chapel open and a fan going behind the curtain where the coffin went, so that the curtain moved a little all through the service as if someone were standing behind it. On the appeal form there was a box that said Grounds for Appeal, with a note underneath that said Please identify any factual inaccuracy in the data used by the service. Lorraine wrote that her father was frightened. She wrote about Mrs. Overton and the medals and the shortbread tin. She wrote that he'd shut the windows because the people who were supposed to make the estate safe hadn't, and that the council had put the sensors on his windows to watch for damp and then used them to say he'd killed himself. She wrote that he wasn't stupid. She wrote that he'd kept a budgie called Bristow for eleven years and never once forgotten to feed it. She ran out of box and carried on onto the back of the page, and Denise didn't stop her, though she knew from the terms of reference that none of it was a factual inaccuracy in the data.

The panel's answer came at the end of September, by email, with a PDF attached. The panel had reviewed the data used by the service and found it to be accurate and complete. The panel had reviewed the service's application of the published methodology and found it to be correct. The panel noted the appellant's submissions regarding the deceased's concerns for his personal security and expressed its sympathy, but observed that such concerns did not constitute a factual inaccuracy within the meaning of its terms of reference and that matters relating to the methodology itself fell outside its remit. Appeal not upheld. At the bottom, in the same friendly font that Proximate used, there was a line saying that if Lorraine had been affected by the issues in this letter, support was available, and a phone number, and Denise rang the phone number later out of curiosity and it was a recorded message about heat safety in the home, which advised listeners to keep windows open at night during hot weather where safe to do so.

Lorraine didn't cry when Denise went round to tell her in person. She'd stopped crying about it by then, she said. She'd moved on to something else. She made them both a tea in her father's mugs, which said WORLD'S BEST GRANDAD and HULL CITY, and she said she'd been reading about the Act on the internet and that there were people saying it had been designed this way from the start. That the threshold and the occupant thing were there so that the money would run out slower, and that the people who'd written it had known that most of the dead would be old people living on their own in flats with windows they were frightened to open, and had known that those people would come in under the line. She said there was a man on the internet who'd worked out that if you took section 4.3 out of the methodology, two thirds of the under-seventies would have gone over. She said she didn't know if he was right. She said it didn't matter if he was right, because nobody who could do anything about it was going to read him.

"Thirty-eight grand," she said. "I don't even want it. I'd have given it to the Stump. They need a new roof on the cafe." She turned the HULL CITY mug round in her hands. "I want the letter. I want the letter that says sorry. That's what I want. That he didn't do it to himself."

Denise didn't have anything to say to that, so she drank her tea.

The heat broke on the second of October. It came in from the west as a long bruise of cloud that sat over the fens all afternoon and turned the light a strange green-yellow, and at about half past five it opened, and for three hours the rain came down on Boston the way Denise remembered it coming down when she was a girl, straight and heavy and warm, drumming on the car roofs in the Market Place and running off the Stump's buttresses in ropes and filling the gutters on Strait Bargate until they spilled over the kerbs. People came out of the shops and stood in it. A man in a suit stood outside the Nationwide with his arms out and his head back like a footballer. Denise watched from the office window on Lincoln Lane and found that she was holding the edge of the sill quite hard.

The Fund put out its end-of-season figures in the third week of October. They were read out on the six o'clock news on Radio Lincolnshire and then on the national news at ten. There had been four declared heat events. The Office for National Statistics estimated excess deaths across the season at around eleven thousand four hundred. Of those referred to the service, two thousand eight hundred and sixty had been certified heat-attributable. The Secretary of State said in a short statement outside the department that every one of those deaths was a tragedy, and that the government had stood by its promise to acknowledge them, and that the Fund had remained solvent throughout the season thanks to the prudent mechanisms built into the Act. She said that the figures showed that the great majority of deaths during hot weather, though deeply sad, continued to be the result of underlying health conditions and individual circumstances rather than climate change itself, and that this underlined the importance of the government's heat-safety messaging. She said, keep your windows open at night where safe to do so. The news put the number in a box on the screen, 2,860, and moved on to the football.

Denise had her own number by then, in the notebook. It was a cheap reporter's pad from the Post Office with a spiral at the top, and she'd started it in July after Pat Holland and kept it going in the van and at the table and at night, a line for every heat death she'd attended in the borough that season, name and age and street and the date and the number that came back. There were seventy-four lines. Twenty-one of them had numbers at or over whatever the line had been that month. Fifty-three didn't. Of the fifty-three, the summaries for forty-six mentioned ventilation, or heating use, or declined support, or occupant contribution. She'd gone back through her photographs and counted the ones with extra locks on the windows: thirty-one. Hook-and-eyes, mostly. A few bolts. One old man in Fishtoft had screwed his windows shut with brass screws, all of them, after a burglary in 2029, and then painted over the screw heads so neatly that you couldn't see them unless you knew to look, and his number had come back fifty-nine.

She didn't know what to do with the notebook. She thought about sending it to someone. She thought about the man on the internet. She thought about Ms. Achebe-Ward, who'd been signed off in September with stress and hadn't come back, and whose replacement was a locum from London who did everything on video. In the end she just kept it, in the drawer of her desk at home, under the takeaway menus.

Lorraine rang her at the start of November to say the council wanted the keys back to Walter's flat. There was a family waiting for it, a young couple with a baby, and the housing officer had been very nice about it but very clear. She asked if Denise would come and help her with the last of it, the big things, because her husband had done his back in and she couldn't face it on her own, and Denise said yes before she'd thought about whether it was her job, because it wasn't, and because she wanted to.

It was a cold day, properly cold for the first time, with a thin wind off the Wash that went straight through her coat. The flat looked smaller with half the furniture gone. The budgie had gone to Lorraine's neighbour's lad. The fan was still in the hall cupboard in its box, and Lorraine took it out and looked at it and laughed, a short sad laugh, and put it by the door for the charity shop. They did the wardrobe and the bed and the settee, which went to the British Heart Foundation van that came at eleven, and then there was just the kitchen left, the table where Denise had sat in August and filled in the drop-downs, and the two chairs, and the window above the sink with its hook-and-eye catch still screwed into the frame.

Lorraine stood looking at it for a while. Then she said, he'll not need that now, and she went to the drawer by the cooker where Walter had kept his screwdrivers, all of them still in a neat row, and she took out a small one with a red handle and unscrewed the hook and the eye, slowly, because the screws were painted in, and put them in her coat pocket. Then she turned the proper handle and pushed the window open, wide, and the cold air came in off the yard and lifted the edge of the old net curtain that was still hanging there, and both of them stood in it for a minute without saying anything.

On the frame, just below where the hook had been, the little white plastic tab of the window sensor blinked once, green, the way it did when it registered a change. Somewhere in Reading the flat's record updated. Denise knew, because she had read the methodology, that the sensors on a void property stayed live until the new tenancy began, so that the council could check the damp, and that the data from them went where all the data went, and was kept. She knew that the record for the flat off Tunnard Street would now show twenty-eight days closed in July and August, and then three months of nothing, and then, on the fourth of November, at twenty past one in the afternoon, in the cold, a window opened all the way.

Lorraine closed it again after a bit, because it was freezing, and they finished the kitchen and locked up, and Denise gave the keys to the housing officer at the office on West Street on her way back to Lincoln Lane. That night she got the notebook out from under the menus and found the line for Walter Sneath, eighty-one, Tunnard Street, eleventh of August, sixty-four, and after the sixty-four she wrote, in her fence-in-a-gale hand, window open 4/11, and the time. She didn't know why. It wasn't anything anyone had asked for. Then she put the notebook back, and turned the heating down a degree, and went to bed, and in the morning before she left for work she went round the house and checked that all the windows were shut, because the forecast said frost, and because she had started, that autumn, to check.
