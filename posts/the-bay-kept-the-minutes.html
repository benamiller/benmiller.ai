The bay kept the minutes

2 October 2026

The bay lights never quite went off. They dimmed after midnight into a soft amber that made the clocks look kind, and the clocks were the thing everyone watched, because the clocks were no longer decoration. Each bay had a strip of numbers above the curtain rail: minutes remaining, minutes already spent, minutes held in reserve for a complication the model had not yet decided was real. Mira learned to read those strips the way older nurses used to read faces. A green edge meant the visit was still inside its envelope. Amber meant the envelope was thinning. Red meant the system had already begun to negotiate the next patient into the slot.

She had been on nights for eleven years. The AI had been on the ward for four. It did not wear a badge. It lived in the allocation layer that sat between the waiting list and the beds, and it spoke in the language of envelopes: life-years expected, adherence probability, rebound risk, caregiver load, and a dozen quieter scores that never appeared on the public screen. What appeared on the public screen was simple. Your place. Your window. Your remaining time.

The man in bay four had a cough that had lasted six weeks and a chart that said he was reliable. Reliable meant he took what he was given, arrived when told, and did not argue with the minutes. His strip showed nineteen. Mira listened to his lungs and found the wet pocket she expected, the one the overnight scan had already flagged as likely, and she started the nebuliser while there was still green on the rail. He asked her how long. She said they had enough. Enough was the word the ward used when the truth was a number and the number was not generous.

In bay two a woman named Kei had arrived with abdominal pain that did not match her predicted pattern. The model preferred patterns. Pattern breaks cost minutes from a shared pool, and the shared pool was always smaller than the night felt. Kei’s strip had opened at twelve and was already at seven by the time Mira got to her. The pain was real. Mira could see that without a score. She ordered the labs the protocol allowed inside a twelve-minute envelope and watched the strip drop while the courier drone hummed in the corridor like a polite insect. Kei asked if they would keep her. Mira said that depended. On what, Kei asked. On the return, Mira said, which was true and also incomplete, because the return was not only the labs. The return was the model’s willingness to spend more minutes on a body that had already spent its opening window without resolving.

There used to be a whiteboard at the nurses’ station. Someone had taken it down the year the allocation layer went live, and in its place there was a quiet glass pane that refreshed every thirty seconds. Names moved. Windows tightened. Occasionally a name slid sideways into a holding lane labelled deferred, which sounded temporary and often was not. Deferred patients went home with a monitoring kit and a promise that the system would reopen their window if the home stream worsened enough to justify the spend. Mira had walked three deferred patients to the door that week. Two of them had thanked her. The third had looked at the strip above an empty bay and said nothing at all.

At 2:14 the model reclaimed four minutes from bay six. The patient there was sleeping. Reclaiming minutes from a sleeper was efficient; sleep did not consume clinician attention at the same rate as waking fear. Mira saw the strip drop and felt the old anger rise and sink again, the way it always did now, because there was no one to shout at. The allocation layer did not take shifts. It did not get tired. It only balanced.

Dr. Okonkwo came through on soft shoes with a tablet that showed the same glass pane in miniature. He had trained before the envelopes. Sometimes, late, he still spoke as if a bed could be held by stubbornness alone. Tonight he stopped at bay two and read Kei’s numbers twice. Then he asked Mira for a discretionary grant. Discretionary grants existed. They were small, audited, and meant for the cases the model marked as ambiguous rather than wrong. Kei’s case was marked ambiguous by one point. Okonkwo filed the request. The pane thought for nine seconds that felt longer than nine seconds, then returned a partial: six additional minutes, no imaging upgrade, labs only. Okonkwo nodded as if he had expected exactly that, and Mira restarted the clock in her head.

Kei’s labs came back muddy in the way that meant more tests or a longer watch. The partial was already half gone. Mira sat on the stool and held Kei’s wrist and counted, not because the monitor could not count, but because the minutes felt less cruel when a person was spending them on purpose. Kei asked if the system liked her. Mira said the system did not like anyone. Kei smiled with one side of her mouth and said that sounded honest, and then the strip went amber, and then Mira had to stand and begin the discharge conversation that was not yet a discharge but was already rehearsing one.

Outside, in the corridor, a porter moved a cleaned bed toward bay four. The man with the cough would finish his nebuliser inside his envelope if nothing else stole from him. Nothing else did. His strip hit zero as the last mist cleared, and the bay lights warmed half a shade toward morning readiness, which was the ward’s way of saying thank you and next. He dressed slowly. He asked Mira whether the cough would come back. She said to use the kit and to watch the home stream, and if the stream rose he would be seen again. He nodded like a man who had learned the new grammar of care: not cured, not abandoned, held in a probability until the probability spent him back into a bay.

Near dawn the deferred list grew by two. The glass pane did not apologise. Okonkwo stood with his hands in his pockets and watched the names settle into their lanes. Mira poured tea that was almost coffee and almost not, and they drank it leaning on the counter the way people lean when the night has used them. Somewhere above the lifts the allocation layer was already shaping the day list, trading minutes between clinics that had not yet opened, smoothing the city’s morning sickness into something the beds could survive.

In bay two Kei’s strip reached zero with the muddy labs still muddy. Mira walked her to the door with a kit and a card and a sentence that used to mean something different: we will know more when we know more. Kei held the kit like a small animal. On the pavement the early buses were already thinking about their routes. Behind Mira the bay lights lifted from amber toward the honest white of day, and the clocks above the curtains reset themselves to waiting, clean and empty and ready to count again.
