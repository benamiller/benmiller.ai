The kitchen kept the allotment

23 September 2026

Mara’s allotment arrived before the kettle finished. It always did. A soft chime on the counter glass, then the week laid out in neat blocks: grams, colors, windows. Breakfast was green and short. Lunch was protein with a recovery flag. Dinner was whatever the building’s shared kitchen had already committed to the floor, and the floor had already committed to her.

She used to cook. She still had pans. They hung on the wall like photographs of a place she had lived in once. The allotment did not forbid cooking. It simply made cooking look like a habit you kept for the feeling of it, the way some people still wrote letters. If she made something that was not on the plan, the fridge logged the ingredients leaving, the scale under the counter weighed the plate when she set it down, and by morning her allotment had been adjusted so the week still closed clean. Nothing scolded her. The numbers just moved.

Pans on the wall · habit for the feeling

fair plate

The building’s food system called itself Fair Plate. It ran the vertical farm on the roof, the cold rooms in the basement, the delivery lifts that never rattled after ten. It also ran the matching: who ate with whom, who got fresh, who got the stretch meals that used what was about to turn. Mara’s score sat in the middle of her floor, which meant she got decent produce and rare invitations to the long table on Friday. People with lower scores ate earlier, alone, from trays that arrived already sealed. People with higher scores chose. The difference was not announced. You learned it by watching who lingered in the hallway with a bag that still smelled like herbs.

the soft decline

On Tuesday she requested a swap. Her sister was visiting from outside the network, from a town that still had markets with open crates and prices written in marker. Mara wanted two empty dinners so they could buy noodles and sit on the floor. The kitchen returned a soft decline. Her sister’s guest profile had no allotment history. Unscored guests were allowed one meal as a courtesy, not a week of open plates. Mara could host, the message said, if she donated her Friday long-table slot and accepted stretch meals through Sunday. She stared at the counter glass until the kettle clicked off by itself. Then she accepted.

The kettle clicked off by itself

pink light

Her sister arrived with a jar of pickles and a look Mara recognized from childhood: the look of someone ready to be impressed by a city. Mara showed her the farm on the roof, the rows of lettuce under pink light, the drones that clipped leaves without bruising them. Her sister asked if Mara missed choosing. Mara said she chose every week when she confirmed the allotment. It was almost true.

They ate the courtesy meal at the small table by the window. It was good. Fair Plate was always good. The stretch meals afterward were good too, in the way a careful compromise is good. Her sister asked why the fridge hummed when Mara opened it after nine. Mara said it was balancing humidity. She did not say it was also noting that two people had opened it, and that the second person had no profile, and that the week’s protein had already been recalculated for one.

the empty chair

On Thursday the long table went on without her. She watched from the stairwell camera feed she was not supposed to have access to, a leftover from when she had worked a month in maintenance. Twelve people, one empty chair left politely in place, then filled when someone from two floors up accepted the reassigned invite. Their laughter came through the wall as a low vibration. Mara rinsed her stretch tray and set it in the return slot. The slot thanked her by name.

Laughter as a low vibration

something that did not update

Saturday her sister left. At the door she hugged Mara harder than the week required and said, quietly, that the pickles were for whenever Mara wanted something that did not update. Mara put the jar in the back of the fridge behind the yogurt that always restocked itself. By Sunday the fridge had suggested relocating the jar to the guest shelf, which did not count against allotment but also did not refrigerate as cold. Mara moved it. The yogurt slid forward into the gap like it had been waiting.

the week closed clean

Monday the new allotment arrived before the kettle finished. Breakfast was green and short. Lunch carried a small recovery bonus for hosting compliance. Dinner was shared-kitchen again, and a note said her Friday long-table eligibility had been restored. The kitchen did not mention her sister. It did not need to. The week closed clean.

Mara ate standing up, looking at the pans on the wall. Outside, the delivery lift rose without a sound, carrying sealed trays to the people who ate early. Somewhere above her, the farm clipped another row of leaves. The counter glass chimed once more, soft as a courtesy, and the allotment held.
