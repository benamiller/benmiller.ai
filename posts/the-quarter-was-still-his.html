Ben Miller

The quarter was still his

Nvidia's first earnings report without Jensen Huang showed $115.8 billion in revenue, and the board used the same call to say he is not coming back. The quarter was still his, but the things he used to decide himself, from China sales to who gets the chips, now go to a board committee, a compliance team and a licence officer in Washington.

18 November 2026

At about quarter past eight on Tuesday morning in California, Jensen Huang posted a long note on X. He thanked the people who had been kind to his family, and he said he had come to the United States 53 years ago and that becoming a citizen was one of the proudest moments of his life. He said he had "made serious mistakes in my personal life" and that he owned them completely. Then he came to the part everyone had been waiting for. "If the Board of Directors decides that my return is in the best interest of our shareholders, our employees, the company, and America, I would be honored to answer these calls and return as CEO." By Wednesday morning the post had more than a million likes and about 45 million views.

Six hours later Nvidia held its quarterly earnings call, and the first person to speak after the investor relations script was not a chief executive. It was Harvey Jones, whom the board had named its first-ever chair six days earlier, and he got to the point in his first minute. "The NVIDIA board unanimously accepted his resignation and does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO." He said the board would take no questions about the circumstances. Then he handed over to Colette Kress, the company's finance chief for the last 13 years and its interim chief executive for the last five days, and her first words were "Our momentum is real."

The momentum was real, and nobody on the call pretended otherwise. Nvidia booked $115.8 billion of revenue in the three months to 25 October, more than double the $57 billion of a year earlier and nearly $7 billion above what analysts expected. It guided to $130 billion for the current quarter and raised its forecast for next year. The shares, which had fallen for six weeks in a row, rose about 4% in after-hours trading.

I read the transcript of the call twice, because I wanted to understand what had actually changed. My answer is that the numbers were Jensen Huang's, built over years of deals and bets he made himself, and almost everything else on the call was a description of how the company will now run without anyone making those deals and bets in that way. The quarter was still his. The rules are new.

It is worth setting down how fast this happened, because it is easy to lose the order.

The story started more than a year ago, with an October 2025 New York Times report linking Huang to Alice Huang, no relation, a former executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore cloud company that was an official Nvidia partner with priority access to its chips. Later reports said US intelligence officials believed she was working for China's Ministry of State Security. The New York Times reported that Secretary of State Marco Rubio had overseen a highly classified memo this spring setting that out, which was then shared among senior administration officials. Megaspeed and related companies are under US criminal investigation over alleged smuggling of American chips into China. Beijing has called the spying claims "purely unsubstantiated." The Justice Department said last week it had not yet reached a conclusion, and President Trump told a podcast on Thursday that "the big thing" was whether she was a spy, and that if she was not, "perhaps it would be different."

On 8 November, a Sunday, Huang told staff he had been unfaithful to his wife. As of Monday the 9th, by The Verge's account, analysts, venture capitalists and even the president expected him to ride it out. On Wednesday the Times reported, citing five people who had worked at Nvidia, a second relationship, with an executive who reported to him. The board met late that night. On Thursday the 12th Nvidia announced that Huang had resigned as president, chief executive and director, effective immediately. The stock fell as much as 16% that day before closing down 4.3%.

What followed on X was something close to a campaign. A letter asking the board to bring him back passed 190,000 signatures, and The Verge counted Elon Musk and Tim Sweeney among the people who signed it. Jason Calacanis wrote on Tuesday that "The WSJ has found Jensen guilty without a trial." Balaji Srinivasan, @balajis, suggested the Department of War could step in under something like the Defense Production Act, "but for the restoration of Jensen Huang." David Senra posted a clip of Shopify's Tobi Lütke saying "There is no replacing a founder like Jensen," and that the company would now be "run by custodians trying and failing to preserve the flame he ignited." On Tuesday afternoon Jeff Bezos told a Semafor conference that if the board did not bring Huang back, it should not hire an outsider.

The board listened to all of it and said no.

Start with the part Huang built, because it is very large.

Data center revenue was $107.6 billion, up 110% on a year ago. Vera Rubin, the rack-scale system that started production shipments in August, brought in nearly $20 billion in its first quarter, which Kress called the fastest product ramp in the company's history. She said the big cloud companies had brought Rubin into production in about a month, half the time it took them with Grace Blackwell. GAAP gross margin was 74.8% and net income was $65.3 billion. Nvidia said its purchase commitments with suppliers had risen to $390 billion, and that the cloud industry's backlog had grown to $2.5 trillion from $2 trillion three months earlier.

The frontier labs are now a line of their own. Kress said she expects the major ones to account for about 15% of Nvidia's data center revenue this year, and in passing she gave Anthropic's annualized revenue at the end of October as $81 billion. That is the company whose shares are due to be priced tonight and to start trading on Thursday. She said that "even in the last week" the largest customers had kept asking her how they would get more supply.

None of this was decided last week. Rubin was designed years ago. The supply agreements that Kress said now stretch to the end of the decade were negotiated by a company Huang ran. Dan Ives of Yorkville Ives wrote in a client note that there had been "a real chance a board five days into an interim CEO would hold back a formal outlook," and that Kress's guidance "speaks volumes about the backlog she inherited." The word that matters in that sentence is "inherited."

This is also the answer to the joke that did the rounds on Tuesday night. @wholemars wrote it as a dialogue: the board says the company is doing great, someone replies that they have just fired the man who delivered those results, and the board says it won't be taking questions on that. It got more than 4,000 likes, and it is fair. The results on Tuesday belonged to Huang, and so will the next few quarters. What the founder campaign got wrong is the idea that the board had to choose between those results and its decision. The results were already locked in by the time the board decided.

The real change on the call took one sentence, in the middle of Kress's prepared remarks, at the end of a paragraph about edge computing and just before one about substrates.

"Q3 sales of licensed Hopper data center products to China was $2.4 billion. Our guidance does not have any licensed Hopper data center products included, and we are no longer shipping licensed Hopper data center products to China."

Those licensed Hopper products are the H200. TechCrunch reported that the arrangement allowing their sale to China was brokered between Huang and President Trump, with the chips routed through the United States so the government could collect a 25% cut. It was the most personal piece of business in the company. Huang had argued for years, against the China hawks in Washington, that American chips should compete in China rather than give up the market. In April he told Dwarkesh Patel that "to concede that market for the United States technology industry is a disservice to our country." On Tuesday the company he founded conceded it, five days after he left, in a sentence that did not mention him.

It may not cost much in the short run. Jacob Feldgoise, a researcher at Georgetown's Center for Security and Emerging Technology, told TechCrunch that China had largely refused to take the H200s anyway, because Beijing wants its labs to buy Chinese chips. The larger question was always the side door.

On Monday the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security issued an interim final rule that requires a licence to ship advanced chips to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand or Brazil, five countries it says carry a "higher-than-normal risk of diversion." Sales to companies headquartered in the United States are exempt, provided they report. Overseas data centers that supply "significant volumes" of compute to companies headquartered in China and other restricted countries now need a licence as well. That is aimed squarely at the practice of Chinese labs renting Nvidia hardware in a third country, which is the business Megaspeed is accused of. Singapore's government said in a statement that it was studying the rule and that it does "not condone companies using their association with Singapore to circumvent or violate the laws of other countries."

Singapore is where the money question sits. Fortune and the Financial Times have reported that Nvidia stopped breaking out Singapore as a separate market in late 2025, when it switched to reporting revenue by customer headquarters rather than billing address. Filings show the Singapore subsidiary booked $117.8 billion in the year to January 2026, about $104 billion of it from outside customers. Nvidia said in a 2025 filing that more than 99% of the data center compute revenue billed to Singapore came from US-based customers who centralise their invoicing there, and it says it has found no evidence of diversion. Culper Research, a short seller, has alleged that more than 20% of Nvidia's compute revenue last year was "driven by China." TechCrunch reported that Jefferies thinks the new rule could take $44 billion to $62 billion out of Nvidia's data center revenue next year.

Kress's answer was that the company would "spend some time studying" the rule, but that with demand running so far ahead of supply, she does not expect it to dent revenue. She may well be right. If every chip that would have gone to a Malaysian rental farm has three American buyers waiting, the top line does not move. What does move is who decides where the chips go. Under Huang that was, to a striking degree, Huang. Under the rule it is a licence officer in Washington.

On Tuesday morning, before the results, Nvidia announced a new standing committee of the board: the National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee. It is chaired by the director Suzanne Nora Johnson, with Stephen C. Neal and John O. Dabiri as members. It will meet at least quarterly, it will hire outside experts in law, national security and trade compliance, and the company's existing national security and trade compliance teams will now report directly to it as well as to Kress. In the release, Jones wrote that "advanced silicon technologies and the emergence of artificial and superintelligence present unprecedented challenges for national security."

It is a dull document, and it is meant to be. Read next to the rest of the call, it describes a company that is replacing a person with a process. A founder who met presidents, decided which neoclouds got chips first and argued Nvidia's case on China personally is being replaced by a committee that meets four times a year and a compliance team that reports to it. When an analyst asked about the company's competitive position during the transition, Jones said that "advanced silicon technologies and national security dynamics are making major changes in our marketplace," and that the next chief executive "will have to navigate those waters very carefully." The board has hired Heidrick & Struggles to find that person and says it will consider insiders and outsiders. Nvidia has also hired Wachtell Lipton, CNBC reports, to investigate whether Huang's conduct compromised national security or company secrets.

The same shift showed up in the money. In the past year Nvidia has become a lender and a guarantor as well as a chip seller. At the end of the quarter its guarantees totalled $118 billion, $105 billion of it credit support for the land, power and buildings at the PORTS-Pike campus, which will be leased to OpenAI for 20 years. Its neocloud agreements came to another $32 billion. CNBC's Kif Leswing wrote that Huang had been "integral in deciding which companies got chips first," and Mizuho's analysts called him the global dealmaker and "AI banker." On Tuesday Kress said the company was "rigorously evaluating future investments, commitments, and guarantees" and would be "disciplined in how we commit capital." Stacy Rasgon of Bernstein spotted a new sentence in the CFO commentary saying some neocloud commitments only take effect once the neocloud has raised its own financing, and asked if that was a change. Kress said it was not new, only newly written down. "Is it new? No. Did we change anything? No."

What she did put first was shareholders. Nvidia returned a record $28 billion in the quarter, $22 billion of it in buybacks. The board approved an extra $150 billion of buybacks during the quarter, the largest authorization in its history, and the company plans to use all $227 billion it has left by the end of next fiscal year. When Jim Schneider of Goldman Sachs asked about priorities, Kress called returning capital a "super top priority."

Sweeney's reply had more than 2,700 likes by Wednesday morning: "Just think of what NVIDIA could do if they invested $150 billion back into their business in America." Calacanis, who had predicted the buyback, wrote that "cfo CEO's are gonna do cfo things!" That is unkind to Kress, but it describes the call accurately. The quarter showed a company run on a balance sheet, by someone careful who is guarding what she inherited and does not want to be the one who loses it.

The best case for Huang's fans was never that the next quarter would collapse without him. It was that the things he did by instinct, like the China bet, the neocloud bet and the willingness to lend a customer the money to buy his chips, are what made the backlog Kress is now drawing down, and that a careful company will not make the next set of bets. Tobi Lütke's word "custodians" is meant as an insult, but it is the right word for what the board has chosen for now. Custodians are what you hire when the person you had became a national security problem.

That is the part of the founder argument that X skipped over. The same instincts that built the business also made it hard to see where the chips went. Nvidia stopped reporting Singapore as its own market in the same year its Singapore arm billed more than $100 billion to outside customers. The woman its chief executive had an affair with had been an executive at a customer that is now under criminal investigation. The Times reported that the Commerce Department paused its part of a US investigation into Megaspeed, and the case moved to the Justice Department, after Nvidia's general counsel contacted its investigators in August 2025. Huang is due to answer questions from several congressional committees in the coming weeks. A board that put him back in charge before any of that is settled would be betting the company's relationship with Washington on his account of it. Jones said, in effect, that the board would not make that bet.

Tonight Anthropic is due to set the price for its listing, at a valuation reported near $1.4 trillion, more than a third of Nvidia's own market value. On Thursday both will trade on Nasdaq. The chip company goes into that week without its founder and without its China business, and with a committee, a buyback and $130 billion of guidance. Kress closed the call with a line that Alex Kantrowitz picked out within minutes as the sound of a company moving on. "Our momentum is real. Our demand is accelerating. Our supply is growing. Our customers and our partners are with us, and employees are committed. We've got this."

She is probably right about the next few quarters, because those were built before she took over. The bets that fill the quarters after that will be made by the committee, the compliance team and whoever Heidrick & Struggles finds, and that is the real test of Nvidia without Huang.
