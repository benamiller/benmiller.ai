The board kept the keys

27 October 2026

The board behind the counter at Mistry Keys and Heels on Drake Street had a hundred and twelve hooks on it, in eight rows of fourteen, and Dev Mistry's father had screwed every one of them in by hand in the spring of 1979, the week before the shop opened, standing on a chair with a bradawl and a tin of brass cup hooks from the ironmonger's on Yorkshire Street that had long since become a vape shop and then nothing at all.

Each hook had a little paper tag tucked behind it, and on each tag, in his father's square capitals, was the name of a shop. Not the business, his father had always said, the shop, because the business could change and the shop stayed where it was, and the landlords who left their spare keys with Suresh Mistry so that somebody local could let the gas man in, or the alarm engineer, or a new tenant on a Saturday when the agent couldn't be bothered to drive over from Manchester, wanted to know that the key for 41 Drake Street would still be on the hook for 41 Drake Street whoever was paying the rent.

When Dev was a boy every hook had had a key on it and most of them had had two, and the board had looked, from the far side of the counter where the customers stood, like a tree in late summer, heavy and full and faintly jingling whenever a bus went past outside. He used to sit on the stool by the heel bar after school and listen to his father on the phone, saying, yes, I've got it, yes, I'll walk it round, no, no charge, and then taking a key down and putting on his coat and going out into the street, and Dev understood without anybody telling him that this was the part of the job his father liked best, more than the cutting or the soles or the engraving of tankards for retirements, because it meant walking up Drake Street with a key in his pocket that opened somebody else's door.

By the time his father died, in the winter of 2019, the board looked different. The keys were still there, mostly, but the shops they opened weren't, or rather the shops were and nobody was in them, and the hooks for the ones that had been bought up and knocked through or boarded or simply forgotten by owners in places like Jersey and Guernsey and a business park outside Reading had begun, one by one, to hang with keys that nobody ever came for. Dev called them the dead keys, though never in front of his mother. There were sixty-one of them by the end, sixty-one hooks with a key on and a tag behind and a door somewhere on Drake Street or Yorkshire Street or the Esplanade that hadn't been opened, as far as anybody knew, since before the pandemic.

He kept the notebook going anyway. That was the other thing his father had left him, apart from the board and the shop and a heel bar with a motor that sounded like a lorry changing gear. It was a ledger, a real one, with a marbled cover and a red spine, and in it his father had written down every time a key went out and every time it came back, the date, the time, who for, what for, and whether the lock had been stiff, which it nearly always had. Dev wrote in it the same way, every first Monday of the month, walking the dead keys round in a carrier bag and trying each one in its door so the locks didn't seize, because a seized lock was a day's work and a customer who'd be cross about the bill, and because, if he was honest, he couldn't stand the thought of a door on his street that nobody could open at all.

His daughter Priya thought this was the saddest thing she had ever heard.

"You're going round," she said, the first time she came with him, when she was fourteen and he'd made her because her mother was working a late, "unlocking empty shops. And then locking them again."

"So they work."

"So they work for who?"

He hadn't had an answer for that, so he'd shown her how to lift the door at number 53 by the handle while you turned the key, because the frame had dropped, and she'd done it, and they'd written it in the book together, 53, Monday 4th, stiff, lifted, P.M., and he'd caught her later that evening reading back through the pages from the eighties with her phone face down on the table beside her, which at fourteen was about as close as Priya came to saying she was interested in something.

The letter from the council came in the February of the year she turned seventeen. It came by post, which was how Dev knew it was about property, because the council did everything else by text now, and it was addressed not to him but to The Keyholder, Mistry Keys and Heels, which made him laugh, because it sounded like a job in a fairy tale.

It said that Rochdale had joined the national Meanwhile scheme, along with Oldham and Bury and a hundred and forty other places, and that under the high street rental powers every commercial unit on the borough's central streets that had stood empty for more than a year would be offered, by auction, on a lease of up to five years, whether the owner liked it or not. It said the auctions and the leases and all the paperwork that went with them would be handled by a public system called the Clerk, which was a sort of AI that Dev had heard about on the radio and not paid much attention to, and it said that the Clerk had identified him, from a search of building insurance records and alarm callout logs, as the named keyholder for a large number of the units concerned, and would he be willing to help.

There was a number to ring. He rang it, expecting a call centre, and got the Clerk.

He'd thought it would be like the bank, all pressing two and repeating himself. It wasn't. It had a woman's voice, unhurried and slightly northern, the kind of voice you'd hear behind the counter at a post office that still had a counter, and it knew who he was before he'd said, and it said it was sorry about his dad, because it had seen the change of name on the business rates, and then it asked him whether it was true that he still had keys for sixty-one empty units on four streets.

"Sixty-one," he said. "How did you know that?"

"I didn't, exactly. I had fifty-three from the insurance records and I guessed the rest from the alarm company's callouts going to your number. Sixty-one is better. Can I ask you something else?"

"Go on."

"The owners of nineteen of those units say they haven't been empty. They say they've been in use for storage, or that they've been let informally, or that they were being refitted. I can't go into the units myself and the council can't afford to send somebody round to all of them. I need to know whether anybody has been in them. Do you know?"

Dev looked at the board. Then he looked under the counter, at the shelf where the ledger lived next to the tin of spare blades and a framed photograph of his parents outside the shop on its first day, his mother in a green salwar kameez with a hand up against the sun.

"I know exactly," he said.

It took him and Priya three evenings to photograph the whole ledger, page by page, on his phone, propped on two tins of shoe polish to keep it steady, and the Clerk read every page as it came in, the square capitals and his father's arrows and crossings-out and his own worse handwriting from 2019 on, and it said thank you after each batch in a way that seemed, even to Dev, who didn't hold with getting soft about machines, to mean it.

What the ledger showed was that nobody had been in nineteen of the units except Dev, once a month, to turn a key and lift a door and write stiff in the margin, for anything from three years to eleven. It showed that the unit at 22 Yorkshire Street, which its owner said had been in continuous use as a store for a furniture business, had last been opened by anybody but a Mistry in October 2016, when the alarm engineer took out the panel. It showed a man from a firm in Reading coming up once, in 2021, and staying eleven minutes. It showed, in his father's hand, in 2014, 41, Saturday, NEW TENANT, CANCELLED, and nothing after that except Dev's monthly ticks.

The owners' solicitors argued, and the Clerk answered them, in letters that it sent copies of to Dev and that he read in the evenings with a mug of tea, which were polite and short and never once made a point it couldn't back with a date. The ledger went to the tribunal in Manchester as evidence, the actual book in a padded envelope, and came back three weeks later with a little sticker on the spine that said EXHIBIT DM/1, which Priya thought was the best thing that had ever happened to the family and photographed and put on her phone's lock screen.

In April the Clerk rang him on a Tuesday and said that all sixty-one units had been cleared for auction, and that the first twelve would go on Friday, and asked whether he would be free on Saturday morning to change some locks.

"How many?" he said.

"Twelve, if you can."

He hadn't changed twelve locks in a month since before Priya was born.

The auctions weren't what he'd pictured either. He'd imagined something like the ones on daytime television, men with clipboards and a gavel and the units going to whoever had the deepest pocket, and he'd assumed, because that was what happened, that the deepest pockets would be the same people who'd left the units empty in the first place, or people like them, a chain of coffee shops, a bookmaker, a firm that bought leases and sat on them. But the Clerk ran the auctions itself and it ran them in a way he didn't entirely follow even after Priya explained it twice, where you didn't just bid money, you told it what you meant to do with the shop, in your own words, and it worked out the rest of what you'd need, the insurance and the fire check and the rates relief and the licence if you were going to sell food or cut hair, and it put all of that into one page that you could read in the time it took to drink a coffee, and then the council's panel and the bids and the Clerk's own reckoning of what the street needed went together into a choice that came out on Friday afternoon and that you could argue with, if you wanted to, by writing back.

The rent was cheap, cheaper than Dev had paid for anything since the eighties, and for the first year it was cheaper still, and the lease said that if you didn't make it you could walk away after six months owing nothing, and that was the bit people kept reading twice when they came into the shop afterwards with the page folded in their coat pockets, because none of them had ever seen a lease like it.

The first tenant he met was a lad of nineteen called Kian Wolstenholme, who'd been fixing games controllers in his bedroom in Kirkholt for three years and selling them on the internet, and who'd bid on number 53, the one with the dropped frame, because, he said, it was the only one he could see from the tram stop.

Dev showed him how to lift the door while you turned the key. Then he took the old barrel out, which came apart in his hand in a little shower of rust, and fitted a new one and a new keep and planed the bottom of the door so it would close without lifting, and while he did it Kian stood in the middle of the empty shop with his hands in the pocket of his hoodie and turned round very slowly, looking at the walls, like somebody in a cathedral.

"It's massive," Kian said.

It was about the size of Dev's front room.

He cut three keys, one for Kian, one for Kian's mum, who was a guarantor and very worried about it, and one for the board. He took the old dead key off hook forty-four and put it in the drawer where he kept the keys that didn't open anything any more, and hung the new one in its place, bright and sharp and smelling of brass, and wrote in the ledger 53, Saturday 19th, NEW TENANT, K. Wolstenholme, controllers, fixed frame, and underlined NEW TENANT twice, the way his father would have done.

There were eleven more that Saturday and nine the Saturday after that. A woman called Ola Nowak took 22 Yorkshire Street, the furniture store that hadn't been a furniture store, and turned it inside a fortnight into a place that sold pierogi out of the front window in paper trays, and she paid Dev in pierogi because she had run out of money buying a second-hand chiller, and he let her, because they were very good and because she cried a bit when the new lock went in, and it seemed rude to give her an invoice after that. A retired teacher called Jean Haworth took the smallest unit on the Esplanade to sell her late husband's records, of which there were apparently eleven thousand, and asked Dev whether he thought it was mad, and he said yes, and she said good. Two brothers from Milkstone took number 41, the one in his father's book with NEW TENANT, CANCELLED written against it in 2014, and opened a barber's, and the older brother, Faisal, read the line in the ledger when Dev showed him, and went quiet, and then asked if he could take a photograph of it to put on the wall by the mirror.

It wasn't all good. A man took a unit on Drake Street to sell phone cases and was gone in nine weeks and the Clerk let him go without a penny owing, the way the lease said, and put the unit straight back up, and Dev changed that lock twice in a summer. A woman who'd meant to do candles found out from the Clerk's one page what the fire check would cost her for the wax and decided, sensibly, to do candles from home, and the unit went instead to a man who repaired sewing machines and was, by some distance, the oldest new business owner in the borough, being eighty-one.

The Clerk rang Dev most Fridays now, not about the locks so much any more, just to check in, the way you'd check in with a neighbour. It told him things. It told him that it had noticed people walking from the tram stop up Drake Street to the bus station at a particular pace, faster than on other streets, the way people walk when there's nothing to look at, and that since Kian's window had started showing a controller in pieces on a little turntable people had begun slowing down outside number 53 for an average of four seconds, and that four seconds was, in the Clerk's experience across a hundred and forty towns, about the start of something. It told him it had suggested to the council's highways people that they take out the guard railing outside numbers 30 to 46, which had been put in after an accident in 1994 and had been keeping people off that side of the pavement for thirty years, and that the council had said yes, which it seemed quietly pleased about.

"You're like my dad," Dev said one Friday, without meaning to, while he was regrinding a set of skate blades for a girl from the ice rink. "He used to walk round and look at everything."

"I can't walk round," the Clerk said. "That's why I ring you."

By September there were fourteen dead keys left on the board, and Dev had stopped walking them round once a month, because by then he was walking up and down Drake Street most days anyway, with keys in his pocket that opened people's doors, letting in the gas man and the alarm engineer and on one occasion a man with a small crane who had come to deliver a wood-fired pizza oven to the unit at the bottom end that had been, for eleven years, a tanning salon that never opened.

People stopped him in the street. It was the strangest part. For years nobody on Drake Street had said hello to him except the woman from the Greggs and a man who slept in the doorway of the old Woolworths and asked him every morning if he had a pound, which he often did. Now he couldn't get from one end to the other without somebody asking about a stiff latch or a window lock or whether he'd cut them a key for their mum, and Ola waved from her window, and Jean Haworth, who had sold, by her own count, nine of the eleven thousand records and was beginning to panic about running out, made him come in and listen to something by a Danish jazz trumpeter whose name he never caught, and said, listen to that, Dev, listen to that, he's walking up a street in it, and he stood among the crates and did listen, and found, to his surprise, that he could hear what she meant.

Priya had started coming into the shop after college. She said it was for the wifi. She sat on the stool by the heel bar, where he had sat when he was a boy, with her laptop on her knees, and she watched him take keys down off the board and hang new ones up, and he noticed that she'd started reading the ledger again, not the old pages this time but the new ones, his own scrawl, NEW TENANT after NEW TENANT, and that sometimes she'd write in it herself, in her small upright hand, when he was busy with a customer and someone came in for a key.

In October she went to the Clerk.

She didn't tell him she was doing it. He found out because the Clerk told him, on the Friday, in a careful voice, that his daughter had made an application for the unit next door to his own, number 15, which had been a building society, then a charity shop, then a phone repair place that had gone the way of all phone repair places, and which had been empty for four years, and whose spare key, it happened, was on hook number two, at the top left of the board, where his father had hung it in 1979 because the building society manager had been his very first customer.

"I can't tell you what she's applied to do," the Clerk said. "That's hers to tell. But I thought you ought to know before Friday, since you'd be the one changing the lock."

He didn't say anything to Priya all week. It was one of the hardest weeks of his life, in a small way. He watched her on the stool by the heel bar and she watched him back, and on Thursday evening, while he was locking up, she said, very casually, "You know number fifteen?" and he said "Which one's that?" and she threw a tin of Kiwi polish at him, and he caught it, and they both laughed so much that a man walking past outside stopped for, Dev reckoned afterwards, considerably more than four seconds.

What she'd applied to do, it turned out, was the ledger.

Not his, exactly. She'd been talking to the Clerk for weeks, she said, after college, about the book, about how it had got all sixty-one units through the tribunal when the insurance records and the alarm logs and the council's own surveys couldn't, because it was the only thing anyone had that said, day by day and in a hand you could check, that a door had or hadn't been opened, and she'd asked the Clerk whether other towns had anything like it, and the Clerk had said, mostly no. Mostly in other towns there was no Mistry, and no board, and no man going round on the first Monday of the month with a carrier bag full of dead keys. Mostly the keys were in a drawer at an agent's in Manchester or Leeds or nowhere at all, and the Clerk had to guess.

So Priya wanted to train keyholders. Locksmiths, shoe menders, the people who ran the last hardware shop on a street, the woman who did the alarm callouts, anyone who held keys for empty buildings, in a hundred and forty towns, and teach them to keep a book like her grandad's, a real one, paper, with a marbled cover if they liked, and walk the keys round, and photograph the pages for the Clerk every month, so that when the auctions came there was proof, and so that the doors worked when somebody finally needed them to.

She'd bid for number 15 because she needed somewhere to run the courses from, and because it was next door, and because, she said, standing in the empty building society on the Saturday morning with her arms folded while he took the old lock out, she wasn't having anyone else have the hook.

The lock was the original. He could tell from the barrel, an old Union mortice from the seventies that his father must have fitted himself, and it came out stiff but whole, and he stood for a moment holding it in his palm in the dusty light from the front window, which still had the ghost of the building society's name on it in letters that had been scraped off and showed only when the sun was low.

"Do you want it?" he said. "For the wall."

Priya took it from him and turned it over and looked at it the way Kian had looked at the walls of number 53, and then she put it in her coat pocket without saying anything, and he fitted the new one and cut her three keys, one for her, one for her mother, and one for the board.

That evening, after she'd gone home, he took the key for number 15 off the counter and went to hang it on hook number two, at the top left, and found that he couldn't, because there was a key already on it, the old dead one, which he had forgotten to take down.

He took it down. He put it in the drawer with the others, the keys that didn't open anything any more, which was getting full now, too full for the drawer to shut properly, so that he'd have to find a box for them, or a tin, or something better than a tin. Then he hung the new key on hook number two and stood back.

There were six dead keys left on the board, in among a hundred and six bright ones, and the board looked, from the far side of the counter where the customers stood, like a tree in late summer, heavy and full and faintly jingling as a bus went past outside, and beyond the bus, through the window, he could see the light on in number 53, where Kian was working late with a controller in pieces on the turntable and three people from the tram stop standing on the pavement watching him do it, where the railing used to be.

On Monday a man rang from Burnley. He said he was a locksmith, the last one left in the town centre, and that he'd been given Dev's number by the Clerk, and that he had a lot of keys on a board in the back of his shop, more than he liked to think about, and he'd been told there was a young woman in Rochdale who could show him what to do with them.

"There is," Dev said. "She's next door. Hang on."

He went to the back of the shop, and knocked on the wall, twice, the way his father used to knock on it when the building society manager had left his lights on, and through the brick, faint but clear, Priya knocked back.

He opened the ledger on the counter while he waited for her. 15, Saturday 24th, he had written, NEW TENANT, P. Mistry, and underlined it twice, and under that, in a hand he didn't know he still had, slightly too large and pressing too hard, the way you write when you are trying not to let the pen shake, he had added the word his father had always used when a key went back on the board and the door it opened had someone behind it.

Home.
